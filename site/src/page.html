Open Campus · Why it exists

College still pays. Its price is the problem.

Since 1980 the price of college has risen more than three times as fast as prices overall. Tens of millions of people paid some of it and left with no degree. Meanwhile the knowledge itself has become almost free to deliver. This is the case for a free, open university, and what we are building.

Part I

The curve

For two generations, the price of college has climbed faster than almost anything a family buys, and much faster than what a family earns.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks the price of college tuition the same way it tracks the price of bread. Set both to 100 in 1980 and the gap is hard to miss. By 2025 the tuition index stood at about 1,349. All consumer prices stood at about 391. Median household income, the money that is supposed to pay the bill, stood at about 494.12

{{chart:index}}

Another way to see it: at the average wage of production and nonsupervisory workers, a year of in-state tuition at a public four-year college took about 117 hours of work in 1980-81. By 2025-26 it took about 381 hours.3

Adjusted for inflation, the published price of a year at a public four-year college rose from $3,230 to $12,290, about 3.8 times. Private nonprofit colleges rose from $14,610 to $46,350.4

{{chart:sticker}}

What the sticker price hides, and what it does not

To be fair to colleges, the published price is not what most students pay. Grant aid at public four-year colleges nearly doubled after 2006, and the College Board estimates that average in-state tuition after grants is now about $1,900 a year. Real published prices have also eased since about 2019.4

But tuition is only part of the bill. Housing, food, books and transport do not get grants in the same way, and they do not shrink while you study. The average full-time student at a public four-year college still faces a net cost of about $21,700 a year after every grant. At a private nonprofit college it is about $38,500.4

{{chart:net}}

Where did the money go? One large piece is a shift in who pays. In 1980 students' tuition covered about 21 percent of public colleges' education revenue. By 2013 it covered 47.5 percent, and in 2025 it was still 38 percent. Over the same years the states' share fell after each recession and only slowly came back.5

Part II

The bill

When the price outruns income, the difference is borrowed.

Student loans on Americans' credit reports grew from about $0.48 trillion at the end of 2006 to $1.66 trillion in 2026, almost three and a half times, and larger than every household debt except mortgages and car loans.6 The federal government alone holds $1.72 trillion, owed by 42.3 million people.7 The average federal borrower owes about $40,500.8

{{chart:debt}}

When missed payments were again reported to credit bureaus in 2025, the share of student loan balances 90 or more days late jumped from under 1 percent to 7.7 percent in one quarter. In the third quarter of 2026 it was 10.9 percent.6 College Board, from Federal Student Aid data, counts 9.3 million borrowers in default, owing $234 billion.8

A fair counterpoint. Fewer new graduates are borrowing than a decade ago. Among 2024-25 bachelor's recipients at public four-year colleges, 45.5 percent borrowed, down from 59.5 percent in 2014-15, and their average debt fell to $27,980.8 The debt that remains is concentrated, and much of it sits with people the next part is about.
Part III

Who is left

The degree works. The trouble is how many people pay for part of one and never get it.

Begin with the good news, because it is the core of the argument. A bachelor's degree still pays. In 2025 the median full-time worker with one earned $1,578 a week, against $966 for a high school graduate, and was less likely to be out of work.9

{{chart:pays}}

Look at the third bar. People who started college and stopped earn barely more than people who never went. They paid tuition, gave up wages, often borrowed, and got almost none of the premium. There are now 37.6 million of them under age 65. In the latest year 2.1 million more stopped out, while fewer than a million came back or finished.10

Who stops out is not random. Of students who started in fall 2019, 75.9 percent from the richest fifth of neighborhoods had a credential six years later. From the poorest fifth, 50.1 percent. Among part-time students, who are usually working, only 34.1 percent finished.11 Forty percent of part-time undergraduates work 35 hours a week or more.12

{{chart:finish}}

The people most likely to need a cheaper, more flexible way to learn are the people the current system loses.

They say so. In Gallup and Lumina Foundation's survey of adults without a degree who are not enrolled, 56 percent called cost a very important reason, against 21 percent who doubted the value.13 The demand is there. The price is in the way.

Part IV

What was tried

This is not the first promise of free education at scale. It is worth being honest about why the last one fell short.

Around 2012, massive open online courses were going to make elite teaching free for everyone. Six years of data from every HarvardX and MITx course tell a different story. Only 3.1 percent of people who opened a course in 2017-18 finished it. Even among those who said they intended to finish, 15.4 percent did. Those who paid for a certificate finished about half the time.14 Certificates went mostly to learners in the richest countries, and the providers moved toward paid degrees and corporate training. edX was sold for $800 million to a company that later filed for bankruptcy.15

{{chart:mooc}}

Three lessons follow. Free content is not the bottleneck: finishing is. A credential that colleges and employers already accept changes behavior. And the people who most need help are the hardest to keep without structure.

What AI changes, and what it does not

The new ingredient is a tutor that can sit beside every learner. The evidence is promising and specific. In a two-week trial at Harvard, students using a carefully designed AI tutor for introductory physics learned more on a short test than students in an active-learning class, with an effect of about 0.63 standard deviations.16 But in a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students, unrestricted GPT-4 raised practice scores and then lowered exam scores by 17 percent once it was taken away. A version built to guide rather than answer largely avoided the harm.17

{{chart:tutor}}

So the honest promise is not Bloom's famous "two sigma". Realistic tutoring effects on broad tests are about a third of a standard deviation.18 That is still large, and for the first time it can cost nothing extra per learner. It works only if the tutor scaffolds instead of answering, draws on checked answer keys, and the learner is tested without it.

Part V

The answer

Open Campus is a free, open library of university courses for 60 majors, written as data, checked item by item, and tied to exams it does not write.

A college charges for four things at once: the knowledge, the teaching, the proof, and the experience. The first is now almost free to deliver. Colleges spend $8,000 to $20,000 per student per year on instruction alone.19 Serving a static course page to one more learner costs effectively nothing.20 Open Campus separates the knowledge from the price, and leaves the proof to bodies that already grant it.

{{chart:spend}}

How a course is built

  1. 1Start from an outside standardEach course maps by code to an exam or curriculum it does not write: a CLEP or DSST outline, a licensing exam blueprint, a professional body's guideline. Success is measured somewhere we do not control.
  2. 2Write it as open dataReadings, worked examples, practice questions with an explanation for every choice, flashcards, interactive exercises and timed practice exams, under an open license anyone may reuse.
  3. 3Check every item twiceEvery item carries a record of who wrote it and who checked it, and the checker is never the writer. Items on licensed or clinical subjects need a named human reviewer.
  4. 4Re-check with every new modelEach time a better AI model is released, it audits the whole library as a fresh reviewer, and the error rate it finds is published.
  5. 5Teach with guardrailsAn optional tutor runs on the learner's own AI with the course loaded, built to ask before it tells and never to give an answer before an attempt. Practice exams run without it.

Every course and program shows one of six levels, so nothing looks more complete than it is.

What it is not

Open Campus is not accredited, grants no degrees or credit, and is not affiliated with any testing or licensing body. The proof stays outside: a learner can earn college credit through credit-by-exam programs such as CLEP, which offers 34 exams at $97 each, recognized at a passing score by the American Council on Education. Each college decides what it accepts.21 Licensing exams have their own eligibility rules, such as accredited coursework or supervised hours, which no website can satisfy. Pre-medicine is offered separately by AdmissionsAcademy.

The knowledge should be free. The proof should be independent.

Open Campus is under construction and built in the open. The specification, the research behind the catalog and every line of code are public.
